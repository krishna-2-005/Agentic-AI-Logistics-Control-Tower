## Corridor audit

*Generated by `python -m src.ml.audit` — regenerate rather than editing numbers by hand.*

Generated: 2026-08-22 14:37 UTC

### 1. The question the audit can actually answer

Week 1 measured the planner's error and found it one-sided: OSRM under-predicts on 98.3% of legs and the median leg runs at 2.00x plan, 110 minutes over on average. **That already answers “is the planner wrong?” — everywhere, yes.** Testing each corridor against the plan would return the same verdict 1,130 times and rank nothing.

So the audit asks the localisation question instead: *given* a network that runs at twice its plan, which corridors run over by more than this network's own typical leg? Every test below compares a corridor against **the other 26,000-odd legs**, never against the planner's 1.0. The effect size that comes out — `excess_ratio` — reads as “this corridor's typical overrun is N times the network's typical overrun”, so 1.0 means ordinary and 1.5 means half again as bad as an already-bad network.

| Choice | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Grain | OD leg | D-002 — the trip columns are cumulative within a leg |
| Statistic | `log(actual_time / osrm_time)` | the raw ratio is right-skewed (up to 41.5x at a corridor's median) |
| Test | Welch t-test, corridor vs rest of network | unequal n (10–151 against ~26,300), no equal-variance assumption |
| Correction | Benjamini-Hochberg, FDR 0.05 | 1130 simultaneous tests would otherwise carry about 56 invented bottlenecks into a top-20 table |
| Minimum support | 10 legs | D-018 — D-004's floor of 30 legs, revisited on the evidence in §4 |

### 2. What the audit found

| Result | Value |
|---|---|
| Corridors in the network | 2,783 |
| Corridors with >= 10 legs (tested) | **1,130** (40.6%) |
| Legs those corridors cover | 20,739 of 26,369 (78.6%) |
| Differ from the network at FDR 0.05 | **785** of 1,130 (69%) |
| — significantly **slower** (bottlenecks) | **273** |
| — significantly **faster** | **512** |
| Worst corridor's excess ratio | 13.88x the network's typical overrun |

**The error localises.** 785 of 1,130 tested corridors are distinguishable from the network baseline after correction, which is the result the rest of the project stands on: a systematic planner error that varies by corridor is one a model can learn and an agent can act on. Had the gap been uniform noise, Week 4's per-corridor claim would have had nothing to say.

**It localises in both directions, and the report has to say so.** 512 corridors are significantly *faster* than the network — the planner is not uniformly optimistic by a varying amount, it is differently wrong in different places. Calling this a “bottleneck audit” and showing only the slow half would misdescribe what was measured.

#### Top 20 bottlenecks

Ranked on **effect size, not p-value**. A p-value grows with support, so ranking on it would put the busiest corridors on top however mild their overrun. Significance decides who is on the list; `excess_ratio` decides the order.

**Read the leg column before the effect size.** With 1,130 corridors tested (D-018), the single largest `excess_ratio` in the family is by construction the likeliest of all of them to be a lucky sample — winner's curse, and it bites hardest at exactly the rows a reader looks at first. **Lowering the floor changed which India the table is describing, and that is a finding rather than a side effect.** The 30-leg table was a metro table — Maharashtra (11), Telangana (3), West Bengal (2), Gujarat (1) — with 4 of its 20 rows beginning and ending in the same city, and it read as a story about urban congestion. ### 4. The support threshold — D-004 revisited

D-004 fixed the minimum at 30 legs in Week 1, in the abstract, before any significance test existed — and asked to be revisited once one did. Re-running the whole audit at each threshold (aggregate, Welch, and a fresh BH correction over whatever family the threshold defines) gives this:

| Min legs | Corridors tested | % of corridors | % of legs covered | Significant | Bottlenecks | Worst excess ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 **(D-018 — decided)** | 1,130 | 40.6% | 78.6% | 785 | 273 | 13.88x |
| 20 | 268 | 9.6% | 33.4% | 199 | 78 | 4.08x |
| 30 *(D-004, superseded)* | 99 | 3.6% | 18.9% | 70 | 34 | 1.92x |
| 50 | 33 | 1.2% | 9.8% | 23 | 11 | 1.54x |
| 100 | 8 | 0.3% | 3.5% | 7 | 1 | 1.17x |

**The threshold was not costing coverage so much as costing the finding.** At 30 legs the audit speaks for 18.9% of the network's legs and its worst corridor runs 1.92x the network's overrun. At 10 legs it speaks for 78.6% of legs, finds 273 bottlenecks instead of 34, and the worst runs **13.9x**. The corridors that are genuinely broken are mostly rare corridors, and a 30-leg floor removed them before the test ran.

The share of tests that come back significant barely moves (70% at 10 legs against 71% at 30), so the looser threshold is not buying significance with noise: Welch is valid at n = 10 and the comparison group is the whole 26,369-leg network either way.

**Decided at the Week 2 sync — D-018: the audited set moves to 10 legs, and every ranked row prints the legs it rests on.** `config.MIN_CORRIDOR_SUPPORT` is 10, so the table above and every number in this document are the audit at that floor. D-004 is superseded rather than overturned: its reasoning — that ranking 2,783 mostly-singleton corridors ranks noise — still holds, and a floor is still needed. What the sweep showed is that it was set one notch too high to see the thing it was built to find.

**What the decision costs, kept in view.** Winner's curse at the top of a 1,130-test family is real, and it is why the leg count is now printed in every ranked row and why the 30-leg set is still written to `benchmarks/raw/w2_corridor_audit_support30.csv` rather than dropped. A claim that survives both tables is worth putting in the paper; one that appears only at the top of the loose table is a lead, not a result. The two framings also answer different questions — *how bad does this network get* is the 10-leg table, *what is reliably true of its busy core* is the 30-leg one — and the report carries both.

## Hub friction ranking

Stage 3 emits two friction metrics per hub and ranks on `dwell_share = dwell_min / start_scan_to_end_scan`, leaving the choice for the audit to confirm (D-015). The two rankings genuinely disagree — Spearman 0.48, sharing 8 of the top 20 hubs — so which one is picked changes the leaderboard and cannot be left implicit.

### Confirming it against a column neither metric contains

Comparing the two dwell metrics to each other settles nothing: both are built from the same two columns. The corridor audit is an independent measurement — `excess_ratio` comes from `actual_time` and `osrm_time`, dwell comes from `start_scan_to_end_scan − actual_time` — so each metric can be scored against the corridors leaving the hub. Over the 119 supported hubs that appear as an origin in the audited set:

| Spearman rho | `dwell_share` | `dwell_min` | Wanted |
|---|---|---|---|
| vs mean corridor `excess_ratio` | -0.05 | -0.00 | high |
| vs mean **planned** leg minutes | -0.30 | +0.55 | near zero |

**D-015 confirmed: rank on `dwell_share`.** Raw dwell minutes correlate +0.55 with how long a hub's legs are *planned* to take — a column neither dwell metric is built from, so this is D-015's suspected confound measured from outside rather than argued. A leaderboard on raw minutes would substantially be a leaderboard of hubs that happen to serve long legs. `dwell_share` runs -0.30 against the same column, which is what a roughly fixed per-leg hub cost looks like when it is spread over legs of different lengths.

**And a second finding, which deserves its own line in the report: hub friction is not corridor friction.** Neither metric tracks the overrun of the corridors leaving the hub (-0.05 and -0.00). Time a shipment spends idle at a facility and the planner being wrong about the road between facilities are close to independent here. Two consequences: the India map and the hub leaderboard are two separate claims and must not be presented as one, and Week 3 should carry hub friction as its own feature rather than assume corridor history already contains it.
